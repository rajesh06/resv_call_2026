\begin{abstract}
The seminal paper by James R. Berquist and Richard E. Sherman provided
systematic methods for adjusting claim triangles to account for changes
in claims handling. Those methods highlighted the dependency of future
development on case reserve adequacy and claim settlement rates. The
Berquist-Sherman method relies on restating claim triangles based on the
data - not a model. In this paper, we present a Bayesian approach to
modeling the changes in case reserve adequacy in order to restate the
triangle.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

\begin{quote}
It's tough to make predictions, especially about the future. 
- Yogi Berra
\end{quote}

In an actuarial analysis, we need to assess the predictive value of
historical data for use in modeling the quantity of interest. In ``Loss
Reserve Adequacy Testing: A Comprehensive, Systematic Approach''
(Berquist and Sherman 1977), James R. Berquist and Richard E. Sherman
described various investigations that the actuary should undertake to
understand (potential) changes in operations that might affect the
predictive value of the data. Then, Berquist and Sherman propose
approaches to adjust the data to address distortions. They focus on two
particular changes in the historical data:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Changes in case reserve adequacy
\item
  Changes in the claims settlement rate
\end{itemize}

In each case, they propose an approach to adjust the data to consider
these changes in the estimation of unpaid claims. In this paper, we
focus on the restatement due to changes in case reserve
adequacy.\footnote{Part 2 of this paper will address changes in claims
  settlement rate. I expect to issue Part 2 later in 2026. In addition,
  we limit the scope to the restatement of the triangle, but do not
  address the application of actuarial models to the restated triangle.}

\bs do not include a statistical model (either frequentist or Bayesian)
to infer changes in case reserve adequacy. Effectively, \bs assume that
the data are 100\% signal and 0\% noise. The goal of this paper is to
propose a statistical model to infer changes in case reserve adequacy.

Actuaries generally model relationships using a linear model. A
frequentist approach to fitting that model (e.g., least squares) is
likely to be problematic with a claims triangle, as there are a limited
number of data points relative to the number of parameters. This could
result in overfitting or in situations where the model does not properly
isolate the effect of each independent variable.

While we don't classify \bs as either frequentist or Bayesian
statisticians, we note that the paper places a significant emphasis on
understanding changes in company operations. Such an investigation would
naturally result in priors for use in a Bayesian model. Those priors
also allow for the isolation of variables. In the approach proposed in
this paper, we fit a model for emergence using Bayesian Markov-chain
Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods.

We also hope that this paper serves as a gentle introduction to fitting
Bayesian models via MCMC. The structure of the proposed model is
therefore intentionally simple. We also include much of the \texttt{R}
code in this paper. The complete \texttt{R} code underlying this paper
is available in a \href{https://github.com/rajesh06/resv_call_2026}{code
repository} on the CAS Github site. {[}Note to reviewers, I will move
this to the CAS GitHub upon completion.{]}

\section{Overview of Methodology}\label{overview-of-methodology}

\bs use medical malpractice data to illustrate their procedure. The
underlying assumption of the \bs method is that adjusted development
factors are a function of (i) the change in paid loss, (ii) the change
in the number of open claims, and (iii) the change in adjusted case
reserves.

\bs calculate adjusted average case reserves using only the inflation
rate and the current case reserves. We express the relationship as
follows:

\[
\bar{c}^{'}_{i,j} = {\bar{c}_{n,j}} \times (1+r)^{i-n}
\]

where,

\[
\begin{aligned}
  \bar{c}_{i,j} &= \text{average case reserve for accident year } i \text{ at development age } j\\
i &\in [1, n]\\
f_t &= \text{case reserve adequacy factor for calendar year }t\\
r &= \text{annual inflation rate}\\
\end{aligned}
\]

Effectively, \bs do not measure case reserve adequacy using the data.
The adjusted case reserves are independent of historical observed case
reserves; they are only a function of current case reserves and the
inflation rate. \bs also measure case reserve adequacy separately by
development lag. However, we believe that it's reasonable to assume that
case reserve adequacy is a calendar-year measure that affects all
accident years/lags. Similarly, \bs present inflation as an accident
year measure. However, because they apply the same inflation rate to all
accident years, the \bs treatment is equivalent to a view of inflation
as a calendar-year effect, which we believe is more intuitive.

\begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-raf}{
\begin{aligned}
  \bar{c}_{i,j} &= {\bar{c}_{n,j}} \times (1+r)^{-(n-i-j+1)} \times f_{i+j-1}\\
  \frac{\bar{c}_{i,j}}{\bar{c}_{n,j}} &= (1+r)^{-(n-i-j+1)} \times f_{i+j-1}\\
  f_{i+j-1} &= \frac{\bar{c}_{i,j}}{\bar{c}_{n,j}} \times (1+r)^{(n-i-j+1)}\\
\end{aligned}
}\end{equation}

In the Bayesian framework proposed in this paper, we use the average
case reserves, \(bar{c}\) and \(r\), to estimate \(f\). Unlike \bs, we
treat \(r\) as a free parameter derived from the case reserve data.
\bs provide the following discussion on the derivation of \(r\).

\begin{quote}
The severity trends obtained from the fitting of exponential curves to
the case reserves per open claim from Exhibit B range from + 27.6\% to
34.2\%. with the exception of such averages at 12 months of development.
In contrast, the severity trends derived from the array of paid losses
per closed claim in Exhibit C range from + 6.7\% to 14.3\%. Furthermore,
the traditional approach of estimating the severity trend from the
fitting of an exponential curve to calendar year paid losses per closed
claim produces a trend of + 15.0\% (with a coefficient of determination
of .9793).

In the above example. no evidence was found which supported the notion
that the severity trend for paid losses was inaccurate, and the
indicated severity trend of +15.0\% was close to that experienced by
many malpractice carriers. Thus, severity trends on the order of +30\%
derived from changes in cast reserves per open claim were rejected as
unreasonable and the + 15\% severity trend was selected as being
representative of the underlying trend.
\end{quote}

As a reminder, we employ a calendar year trend assumption, and through
the use of Bayesian fitting we can use priors to control for
unreasonable results.

\section{The Bayesian Model}\label{the-bayesian-model}

We can now turn to fitting the Bayesian model. We first need to identify
likelihood functions for the observed data and priors for each variable
that we need to estimate.

\begin{itemize}
\item
  The ratio of case reserves has a domain of non-negative values. We
  assume that these ratios are Gamma distributed.
\item
  The inflation rate variable has a domain of real numbers, though we
  recognize that negative inflation rates are not common. The prior
  distribution is likely centered a few percentage points
  above\footnote{Actuaries generally expect inflation related to claims
    to exceed general economic inflation.} the underlying rate of
  economic inflation.\footnote{\bs is a 1977 paper. We assume that the
    research and writing occurred in the mid-1970s. The underlying
    inflation rate in that period was both high and volatile.} We select
  a Normal distribution with mean of 15\%, and standard deviation of
  10\%. We define the inflation factor as \(1\) plus the factor to
  inflate the historical case reserves to current cost levels.
\end{itemize}

We therefore assume that the product of the inflation adjustment and
observed case reserve ratios are Gamma distributed. That Gamma
distribution represents reserve adequacy factor.

To provide a more intuitive understanding, we first present the fitting
of the Bayesian model to the observed ratio of average case reserves at
year-end 1975 and average case reserves at year-end 1976. (Year-end 1976
is the current valuation of the data in \bs.) We present the Stan script
for this model below.

\section{Adjusted Triangle}\label{adjusted-triangle}

We can now use the fitted Bayesian models to adjust the data in the
triangles. We have two options to adjust the data:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  We could adjust the observed case reserves by applying the fitted
  reserve adequacy factor.
\item
  We could restate prior diagonals by detrending current case reserves
  using the fitted trend factor.
\end{itemize}

variability. Since we fit a Bayesian model, we have a posterior
distribution for the trend rate and a posterior predictive distribution

\section{Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

We believe that actuaries are generally hesitant to apply
\bs adjustments as they view the underlying assumption of 100\% data
credibility as unreasonable, particularly for small sample sizes. In
this paper, we presented a model for the \bs case reserve adequacy
adjustments. The model infers these changes from the data, rather than
assuming 100\% data credibility. This approach not only provides a
modeled basis for \bs adjustments, but also results in a distribution of
development factors that can be used to develop a distribution of
reserves. Importantly, the method presented detects changes in case
reserve adequacy from the data, rather than assuming that such changes
have occurred. In that sense, the methodology is sufficiently general so
as to be applied even when there are no known instances of case reserve
adequacy changes, or when we want to use a model to test changes in case
reserve adequacy.
